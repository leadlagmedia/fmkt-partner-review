The Investment Thesis

Why free markets matter

The debate is bigger than a single regulatory cycle. FMKT should own a broader category: free markets versus socialism and state-directed economic control. Deregulation remains the practical investment lens, the way the portfolio team evaluates where freer markets may change the opportunity set.

The policy divide investors can understand

The investment conversation is increasingly framed around economic systems: free enterprise, private-sector dynamism, decentralized decision-making, socialism, communism, and the role of government in allocating capital. FMKT should meet that language directly without turning the Fund into a partisan product.

The core idea is economic, not party-political. Free markets rely on competition, property rights, entrepreneurship, price signals, and private capital formation. State-directed economics relies more heavily on government mandates, subsidies, controls, and centralized allocation decisions.

From rhetoric to portfolio construction

Search behavior and voter rhetoric increasingly use words like socialism, communism, capitalism, and free markets to describe economic choices. The website can use that vocabulary as an educational and discoverability frame while keeping the Fund's investor-facing thesis grounded in process, risk, and active management.

That makes the "opposite of socialism" framing useful, but it should be expressed as an economic systems contrast rather than a partisan slogan. FMKT stands on the side of free-market capitalism: private ownership, voluntary exchange, competitive markets, entrepreneurial risk-taking, and capital allocation driven by businesses and investors rather than central planners.

FMKT's role is to express the free-market side of the economic debate through a fund process, not to predict an election outcome or advocate for a political party. The Fund's identity can be sharper than "deregulation-first ETF" while remaining nonpartisan and compliance-aware.

References to socialism, communism, capitalism, and free markets are economic-policy concepts and search-language architecture, not partisan advocacy.
